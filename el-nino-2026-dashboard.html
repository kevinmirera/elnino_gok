The four pathways

Flooding and heat don't cause outbreaks directly — they act through four well‑documented mechanisms. Surveillance teams get more from watching the mechanism than waiting for case counts.

01

Closer contact

Habitat loss and flooding push wildlife — bats, monkeys, rodents — out of forests and toward farms and settlements.

02

Stressed hosts

Displaced, food‑stressed animals shed more pathogen than settled ones, raising the chance of a jump to livestock or people.

03

Spillover

Once species boundaries blur in degraded ecosystems, zoonotic disease — Ebola, rabies, Nipah — has more opportunities to cross over.

04

Vector expansion

Warmer, wetter conditions lengthen mosquito and tick breeding seasons and let them establish in new, previously cooler areas.

Community action

Adapted from the five upstream‑prevention strategies backed by public‑health researchers, translated into things a household, chief, or county team can actually do this season.

Around the home

  • Empty or scrub anything holding standing water weekly — mosquitoes breed in as little as a bottle cap's worth.
  • Clear gutters and drains before the rains peak.
  • Repair door and window screens.

Personal protection

  • Use an EPA/PMI‑listed repellent (DEET, picaridin, IR3535, or oil of lemon eucalyptus).
  • Sleep under an insecticide‑treated net in malaria‑ and dengue‑affected areas.
  • Long sleeves and trousers tucked into socks in tall grass or bushland.

Reporting and surveillance

  • Report unusual animal die‑offs (especially bats) or flooded latrines to local health authorities.
  • Chiefs and community health promoters flag suspected cholera or measles clusters early, not after a spike.
  • Joint human–veterinary–environmental teams screen at flood‑displacement sites.

County coordination

  • Pre‑position ORS, cholera rapid tests, and water‑treatment supplies ahead of the rains.
  • Identify evacuation grounds and confirm road/bridge passability before flooding, not during it.
  • Protect remaining forest cover near settlement edges — it's a buffer, not just habitat.
